01

“Sparse” is only the beginning.

Qwen FP8 leads throughput at five of six workloads. MiMo generates tokens fastest when a request runs alone. V4.1 holds the least live state. Those are three different strengths.

We compared five deployments on the same eight H100 GPUs, using the same public code, math, and chat text, the same vLLM build, and the same serving limits. Each request generated exactly 256 tokens. Separate diagnostic captures inspect attention, feed-forward networks (FFNs), and live key/value (KV) state.

At 1K input and one client, MiMo leads with 160.6 output tokens/s. Qwen FP8 leads the other five points, reaching 412.6 tokens/s at 16K/c8 and 145.1 at 64K/c8. GLM is second at all three eight-client points. But Qwen FP8 and GLM also hold the most live KV per token; V4.1 is the most compact.

Qwen checkpoint update · October 8, 2026. Every Qwen result shown here used the FP8 checkpoint. All future Qwen work will use the original Qwen/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next BF16 checkpoint. BF16 has not been measured in this study. Checkpoint policy and source pin.

Same core setup, with recorded deviations. Qwen FP8 and GLM ran after the first three models; launch order was not rotated across all five. GLM has no thinking-off switch and used reasoning_effort=low; the other four ran with thinking disabled. These limits qualify the comparison, rather than exclude the two models. Declared addendum.

Qwen FP8 and GLM lead under load

At 64K/c8, Qwen FP8 reaches 145.1 tokens/s, GLM 115.7, V4.1 100.5, MiMo 78.5 and 0731 71.5. Qwen FP8’s p50 first-token latency at 64K/c1 is 1.72 s; GLM takes 2.08 s and V4.1 2.28 s.

MiMo keeps the single-client decode lead

At c1, MiMo’s p50 time per output token stays at 5.8–6.0 ms from 1K to 64K. Qwen FP8 takes 6.3–6.4 ms, GLM 6.6–6.7 ms, 0731 7.7–7.8 ms, and V4.1 about 9.0 ms. At c8, TPOT also includes interleaved prefill; it is not isolated decode time.

Keep the uncertainty. GLM’s slow first blocks at 16K/c1 and 64K/c1 remain in the means, as does 0731’s slow 1K/c8 block. Qwen FP8’s timing windows were 46–85 s and GLM’s 59–91 s; some were shorter than 60 s because request counts were sized on the first three. V4.1 and MiMo are within 1% at 64K/c1, while Qwen FP8 leads both. Three repeats are not a production latency study.

The search still costs something

0731’s indexer grows from 6.4 to 41.1 ms between the 16K and late 64K prefill captures. Qwen FP8’s grows from 4.8 to 12.2 ms, GLM’s from 2.0 to 13.1 ms. Selecting fewer positions does not eliminate the cost of finding them.

Recurrent state changes decode

At 64K/B=8, Qwen FP8 and GLM have the lowest attention-path kernel sums: 0.77 and 0.76 ms, versus 1.95–2.14 ms for the other three. Most of their layers update fixed-size state; fewer softmax layers do context lookup.

The cheapest attention does not guarantee the fastest prefill. MiMo has the lowest attention cost here, but Qwen FP8 and GLM spend far less time in expert GEMMs. Qwen FP8’s late-64K expert sum is 10.4 ms, GLM’s 25.1 ms, and MiMo’s 244.0 ms. Their full-model serving advantage cannot be read from an attention-only chart.

Attribution boundary. Dense GEMMs include attention projections and may include other dense work; MiMo’s category includes its layer-0 FFN. 05

The faster models hold more state.

V4.1 uses the least live KV state; Qwen FP8 and GLM use the most. At 64K with one active request, the whole-node estimates are 0.93 GiB for V4.1, 2.14 for 0731, 2.77 for MiMo, 6.85 for Qwen FP8 and 5.77 for GLM. Fast sparse lookup does not imply compact storage.

Sparse reads, growing storage.

Qwen FP8 and GLM keep fixed-size recurrent state in most layers, but their sparse softmax layers still retain growing, uncompressed KV. The 64K snapshots are about 13.16 and 12.01 KiB/token/GPU, versus V4.1’s 1.85. Per-layer hybrid state dtypes are not separately audited here; “auto” is the recorded launch setting.

Pool size changes the trade-off.

At 64K/B=8, Qwen FP8 holds about 56.08 GiB across the node and GLM 47.48 GiB. GLM occupies 20.7% of its smaller KV pool, versus Qwen FP8’s 13.6%. This does not establish maximum request capacity; no saturation test was run.

At short context, fixed state and block rounding raise bytes/token: Qwen FP8’s cache block is 400 tokens and GLM’s 640, aligned with recurrent-state pages. 0731 also pays 256-token block overhead. V4.1 shares KV across layers; its larger weights and host-resident Engram tables are separate costs.

An open question · HBM traffic

Bytes held are not bytes moved.

No hardware HBM byte counters were collected: Nsight Compute was unavailable on the node. These results cannot establish which attention path reads or writes the fewest HBM bytes.

The study’s MiMo estimate at 64K/B=8 implies roughly 2.8 TB/s per GPU if the added live KV is read once per step. It combines allocation and kernel-time estimates; it is not measured bandwidth or proof of a bandwidth bottleneck.

Prefix reuse remains unanswered. Prefix caching was off for every model. Live request state does not establish retained-prefix capacity, reuse behavior or maximum concurrent requests.

Sources: all 30 live-state snapshots ↓ · actual live token counts, pool sizes and weights · HBM assumptions.

06

The expert kernel changes the picture.

Qwen FP8’s experts are much smaller: width 640 on a 2,560-wide hidden state, versus widths 2,048–2,304 in the other models. Its eleven active experts represent roughly 54M weights per MoE layer, compared with 176–248M elsewhere. Expert count alone misses that difference.

GLM makes the kernel question hard to ignore. Its experts have the same hidden/intermediate dimensions as 0731’s and MiMo’s, and it activates nine experts including the shared one. Yet at the late 64K prefill chunk, GLM’s expert sum is 25.1 ms, versus 158.5 ms for 0731 and 244.0 ms for MiMo. Qwen FP8 is lower still at 10.4 ms.

These deployments use different precision and backends. The DeepSeek and MiMo experts use 4-bit HUMMING; Qwen/GLM logs select FLASHINFER_CUTLASS FP8, with the findings identifying a DeepGEMM expert path in traces. Precision, kernels, layer counts and routing all differ. The 6–10× aggregate expert-time gap is a cross-model observation, not a measured speedup from changing one backend.

Sparsity percentage is not a latency prediction. V4.1 routes to only 1.56% of its experts, but has the largest active expert-weight estimate per layer. Qwen FP8 activates 1.95%, yet its smaller experts cost much less in these captures. Shared experts, expert width, depth, batch and the executed kernel all matter.

In decode, Qwen FP8’s expert GEMM grows from about 1.04 ms at B=1 to 1.45 ms at B=8 in the 1K captures, while MiMo grows from 1.44 to 4.87 ms. That is consistent with Qwen FP8’s smaller increase in whole-step time. Routing, activation and combine remain visible costs; they are shown separately from expert matrix multiplication.

Realized routing is still missing for all five models. Tokens per expert, unique experts touched, padding and rank imbalance were not collected. Larger batches may touch more distinct experts, but no measured imbalance or straggler is established. Nominal active parameters are not hardware HBM bytes.

Keep three concepts separate: attention sparsity selects positions, MoE activation sparsity selects experts, and quantization reduces stored precision. None establishes hardware structured weight sparsity. A matched backend ablation would be needed to turn the expert-kernel hypothesis into a causal result.

Sources: expert shapes, formats and loaded backends · five-model FFN component costs · unsupported routing telemetry and proposed ablation.

07

Match the finding to the workload.

Qwen FP8 leads serving throughput. MiMo leads single-client generation. V4.1 uses the least live KV. The five-model comparison makes the speed–memory trade-off clearer.

Qwen FP8
Highest throughput

Leads five of six timing points and all three eight-client points; MiMo wins 1K/c1. Small FP8 experts and mostly recurrent layers are consistent with the trace results. Qwen FP8 also holds the most live KV per token and per 64K request in these snapshots.

GLM
Second under load

Second in throughput at every c8 point and at 64K/c1, with much cheaper prefill expert GEMMs than the DeepSeek/MiMo deployments. It has the largest loaded weights (38.8 GiB/GPU), high live KV, the smallest reserved pool, and no thinking-off switch.

MiMo
Fast single-client generation

Has the fastest single-client TPOT and highest throughput at 1K/c1. Its prefill attention path is cheapest at both sampled contexts, but expert GEMMs are expensive. It holds less state than Qwen/GLM at 64K, and more than either DeepSeek model.

V4.1
Smallest live KV

Has the smallest live-state footprint across the measured snapshots. It leads 16K/c8 and 64K/c8 within the original three models, while Qwen FP8 and GLM are faster across all five. Source and traces verify its prefill skip; single-client generation is the slowest.

0731
Stable decode across context

Its single-client TPOT changes little with context, and its weights are smaller than V4.1’s. It does not lead throughput at any of the six points. Long-context indexing and short-request block overhead remain visible costs.

The evidence is strongest for the recorded serving values. Cross-group order and GLM’s reasoning setting remain comparison limits. Traces suggest mechanisms; a matched single-change experiment is needed to assign a causal speedup. Hardware traffic, routing behavior, quality, saturation and performance beyond 64K remain open.

All five deployments belong to blog-architecture-h100-v1; the Qwen/GLM addendum preserves their declared collection order and deviations.

08

How this was measured.

All numbers here come from the October 7–8 architecture study: 90/90 valid timing runs, or five models × three input buckets × two client loads × three launch-separated blocks. The first three models account for 54 runs, and the declared Qwen/GLM addition contributes 36. Both groups use the same core deployment and request protocol.

HARDWARE & LIMITS

One 8×H100 80GB node. TP8 with expert parallelism, memory utilization 0.90, max context 262,144, max sequences 64, explicit chunk budget 8,192 tokens.

REQUESTS

Balanced public code/math/chat texts, approximately 1K/16K/64K input, 256 forced output tokens, temperature 0. Same texts across models; actual token counts differ. This does not compare natural answer lengths.

EXECUTION & THINKING

Native precision, AR only, speculation off, prefix caching off. DeepSeek uses thinking=false; MiMo and Qwen FP8 use enable_thinking=false. GLM has no off switch and uses reasoning_effort=low; 1/3 natural-EOS checks still included reasoning text.

VALIDATION & SAMPLING

Every timed request completed with 256 tokens. Fixed per-block request counts: 48/204 at 1K, 36/75 at 16K, 18/33 at 64K (c1/c8). Slow first blocks remain in the results.

DIAGNOSTICS

30 captures: 16K/64K prefill and 1K/64K decode at actual B=1/8 for every model. Separate profiler launches, all eight ranks, one capture per point. Another 30 live-KV snapshots cover all context/batch points.

LIMITS

Closed-loop c1/c8 screening, not saturation or production SLO validation. Three repeats do not establish tails or statistical confidence. Only functional task checks, no model-quality comparison. HBM and expert-routing counters unavailable.

LAUNCH ORDER

The first three models rotated order across their blocks. Qwen FP8 and GLM ran afterward, alternating with each other. Order was not rotated across all five, so node drift is confounded with the two groups.

DURATION & TOKENIZATION

Qwen FP8 windows: 46–85 s; GLM: 59–91 s. Counts were sized on the first three. At 16K/c1, Qwen FP8 processes 17,199 mean input tokens versus DeepSeek’s 16,351; the same texts do not imply equal token counts.
