01

“Sparse” is only the beginning.

MiMo generates tokens fastest when a request runs alone. V4.1 gets long prompts ready faster and holds less live state. The cheapest attention does not guarantee the fastest whole model.

We compared three deployments on the same eight H100 GPUs, using the same public code, math, and chat text. Each request generated exactly 256 tokens. We varied input length and client concurrency, then used separate diagnostic captures to inspect attention, feed-forward networks (FFNs), and live key/value (KV) state.

The result is a trade-off. At 1K input and one client, MiMo delivers 160.6 output tokens/s, versus 118.1 for 0731 and 106.0 for V4.1. At 64K and eight clients, V4.1 leads with 100.5 tokens/s, versus MiMo’s 78.5 and 0731’s 71.5. The component evidence helps explain that change: V4.1 skips most prompt-token work in 19 layers; MiMo has a simpler, cheaper decode path.

Read these as deployment results. The checkpoints use different weight and KV formats and different kernels. These numbers describe vLLM 554340f3 on this workload; they do not isolate architecture or establish equal answer quality. The older September results use a different runtime and are not combined here.

MiMo’s single-client advantage

At c1, MiMo’s p50 time per output token stays near 5.8–6.0 ms from 1K to 64K. 0731 takes 7.7–7.8 ms, V4.1 about 9.0 ms. MiMo leads throughput at 1K and 16K with one client.

V4.1’s long-prompt advantage

At 64K/c1, V4.1’s p50 first-token latency is 2.28 s, versus 3.14 s for MiMo and 3.28 s for 0731. At c8, its cheaper prefill helps the whole serving workload; c8 TPOT includes interference from prefill and is not isolated decode time.

Keep the uncertainty. V4.1 and MiMo differ by less than 1% in throughput at 64K/c1; that is too small for a firm winner from this screen. 0731’s slow first 1K/c8 block is retained: 551.9, 621.8, and 622.5 tokens/s (median 621.8). Three repeats are not a production latency study.

Kernel sums are not wall time. Work can overlap across streams; idle gaps also matter. These bars cannot be subtracted from latency to predict an optimization gain. One capture per point; the eight ranks are not eight independent experiments.

0731 pays to find the sparse set

From the 16K capture to the late 64K chunk, its attention core grows from 41.0 to 55.3 ms, but selection grows from 6.4 to 41.1 ms. The indexer becomes almost as expensive as the attention it enables. A bounded selection count does not make the search free.

MiMo pays as full context grows

Its nine full-attention layers account for the rising attention cost. At 64K/B=8 decode, the measured paths are close: 1.95 ms for MiMo, 2.04 for 0731, 2.14 for V4.1. No attention crossover beyond the measured contexts has been established.

Attention alone is not the whole explanation. At 1K/B=1, dense GEMMs cost 3.59 ms for 0731, 2.81 ms for V4.1, and 1.09 ms for MiMo. DeepSeek also has 1.54–1.70 ms in the mHC/residual/norm category. These are plausible contributors to the decode gap, not independently measured causal penalties.

Attribution boundary. Dense GEMMs include attention projections, but MiMo’s category also includes its layer-0 FFN. We therefore show them separately, not as pure attention. Adding all dense GEMMs gives only an upper bound on the projection-inclusive attention path. Unclassified work and communication remain visible. The profiler increased observed decode step time by about 0.4–12% in its overhead controls.

What does a live context cost?

V4.1 uses the least live KV state in these snapshots. At 64K with one active request, the whole-node estimate is 0.93 GiB, compared with 2.14 GiB for 0731 and 2.77 GiB for MiMo. Shared, compressed KV and the native cache format matter more than the advertised token-pool size.

Store less, reserve differently.

V4.1 holds less KV per live token but has heavier resident weights and a smaller reserved KV pool. Its Engram tables also use pinned host memory. Live state, the reserved pool, and total device memory are three distinct quantities.

Short contexts pay for blocks.

0731’s 256-token block allocation and per-request state make its short-context bytes/token much higher. MiMo’s full layers suggest about 5.6 KiB/token/GPU from shapes (9 × 320 × 2 bytes), close to its 5.69 KiB snapshot estimate.

An open question · HBM traffic

Bytes held are not bytes moved.

No hardware HBM byte counters were collected: Nsight Compute was unavailable on the node. These results cannot establish which attention path reads or writes the fewest HBM bytes.

The study’s MiMo estimate at 64K/B=8 implies roughly 2.8 TB/s per GPU if the added live KV is read once per step. It combines allocation and kernel-time estimates; it is not measured bandwidth or proof of a bandwidth bottleneck.

Prefix reuse remains unanswered. Prefix caching was off. Live request state does not establish retained-prefix capacity, reuse behavior, or maximum concurrent requests. No saturation experiment was run.

Sources: live KV CSV ↓ · snapshots, actual live token counts and pool sizes · HBM assumptions.

06

Fewer active experts ≠ cheaper FFNs.

V4.1 activates the smallest fraction of routed experts, yet its active expert weights per layer are the largest. Its hidden state and expert width are larger, and a shared expert runs for every token. A top-k ratio leaves out that work.

Depth changes the total. MiMo runs 47 MoE layers on all prompt tokens. V4.1 trims work in 19 later layers, so its per-chunk expert time is lower despite larger experts. All three use the HUMMING expert backend in this build, with FP4 or MXFP4 weights.

The router is visible at small batch. At 1K/B=1, routing, activation and combine account for approximately 40–45% of the traced FFN path (expert GEMM plus that category). Expert GEMM itself is similar across the models: 1.29–1.49 ms. Expert count alone does not explain the overall decode ranking.

Realized routing is still missing. Tokens per expert, unique experts touched, padding and rank imbalance were not collected. Larger batches may touch more distinct experts, but we cannot claim that a measured imbalance or straggler caused these timings. Nominal active parameters are not hardware HBM bytes.

Keep three concepts separate: attention sparsity selects positions, MoE activation sparsity selects experts, and quantization reduces stored precision. None implies hardware structured weight sparsity. The measured expert cost motivates a backend ablation; it does not establish that changing kernels will improve performance.

Sources: expert shapes, formats and active-weight arithmetic · FFN component costs · unsupported routing telemetry and proposed follow-ups.

07

Match the finding to the workload.

The useful question is which work each deployment avoids, and when that saved work matters.

MiMo
Fast generation

Leads at 1K for both loads and 16K/c1; has the fastest single-client TPOT. Its simpler path is consistent with that advantage. It pays more live KV than either DeepSeek model at 64K and spends substantial prefill time in expert GEMMs.

V4.1
Long-prompt efficiency

Leads throughput at 16K/c8 and 64K/c8 and uses the least live KV. Source and traces verify the prefill skip. It has the slowest single-client generation and the largest loaded weights of these three.

0731
Stable single-client decode

Its TPOT changes little with context, and its weights are smaller than V4.1’s. It is not the throughput leader at any of the six measured points. Long-context indexing and short-request block overhead remain visible costs.

The evidence is strongest for serving differences at the measured points. Kernel traces explain plausible mechanisms; a matched single-change experiment would be needed to assign a causal speedup. Hardware traffic, routing behavior, quality, saturation, and performance beyond 64K remain open.

08

How this was measured.

All numbers here come from the October architecture study. Its three-model timing subset contains 54/54 valid runs: three models × three input buckets × two client loads × three launch-separated blocks. Model order rotated across blocks, which ran in one evening.

HARDWARE & LIMITS

One 8×H100 80GB node. TP8 with expert parallelism, memory utilization 0.90, max context 262,144, max sequences 64, explicit chunk budget 8,192 tokens.

REQUESTS

Balanced public code/math/chat texts, approximately 1K/16K/64K input, 256 forced output tokens, temperature 0. Same texts across models; actual token counts differ. This does not compare natural answer lengths.

EXECUTION

Native precision, autoregressive decoding, speculation off, prefix caching off. Thinking disabled with thinking=false for DeepSeek and enable_thinking=false for MiMo.

VALIDATION & SAMPLING

Every timed request completed with 256 tokens. Fixed per-block request counts: 48/204 at 1K, 36/75 at 16K, 18/33 at 64K (c1/c8). Slow first blocks remain in the results.

DIAGNOSTICS

18 captures: 16K/64K prefill and 1K/64K decode at actual B=1/8 for each model. Separate profiler launches, all eight ranks, one capture per point. Another 18 live-KV snapshots cover all context/batch points.

LIMITS

Closed-loop c1/c8 screening, not saturation or production SLO validation. Three repeats do not establish tails or statistical confidence. Only functional task checks, no model-quality comparison. HBM and expert-routing counters unavailable.
